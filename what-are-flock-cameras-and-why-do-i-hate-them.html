What are Flock Cameras and Why Do I Hate Them?

I hate these cameras. If you're unfamiliar with my content or who I am, you may be wondering: why? If you're one of my friends and family you're probably thinking, "Oh great, he's complaining about these cameras again." Now hey, before you roll your eyes and close this page, just hear me out.

What if I told you that these cameras were going to usher in the end of the world? Well, maybe not quite that, but I do think what these cameras symbolize can be very dangerous for average blokes like you and me. But before I get into that, let's take a couple of steps back.

What is a Flock camera?

In Flock's own words, "Flock license plate readers are motion-activated cameras paired with cloud-based software built specifically for public safety and security."

AKA they are ALPRs, or Automatic License Plate Readers. ALPRs use technology like Optical Character Recognition which captures the text of your license plate, then logs it with the exact time and location alongside additional photos of your vehicle. Specifically, Flock cameras use what they call "Vehicle Fingerprint" which also notes dents, scratches, color, stickers, roof racks, and of course, YOU.

Where Flock differs from typical ALPRs is the aforementioned cloud-based software, which allows them to do so many fun things with your data. For no cost at all, you get to be added to a nationwide database owned by a private company that pretty much anyone can use with a subscription. Don't even get me started on subscriptions right now. You can be added to alerts so that whenever you enter or leave an area, whoever has access will be notified. And TRUST ME, that will never be abused by law enforcement! You could almost swear we are living in a whole other year.

So what should you be looking out for? Here is the current lineup of hardware watching your every move:

  • The Flock Falcon: This is the all-around mass surveillance GOAT. The standard model is the one you'll see in your city, town, and occasional country road. It takes rapid-fire photos of passing traffic.

  • The Falcon LR (Long Range): Predominantly used in high-speed areas such as highways and high-traffic intersections. It is optically optimized with a farther focal depth to pull data on fast-moving cars or multi-lane traffic without losing image fidelity.

  • The Sparrow: A stripped-down Falcon that only does basic ALPR functionality. The Sparrow is usually reserved for rich freaks in HOAs or gated communities. (Flock also chucks ALPRs on mobile trailers for temporary deployment).

  • The Condor: This camera may look smaller, but don't be fooled. While the Falcon only takes photos, these little suckers stream live video. There are two variants of this: a smaller fixed Bullet design and a PTZ (Pan-Tilt-Zoom) design with AI tracking that can automatically or manually follow cars, bikes, animals, or the unsuspecting person.

  • Drones: Oh yeah, they also make drones now. These autonomous, rapid-response UAVs are designed to integrate directly with the existing ground camera network. When a street-level camera flags a specific vehicle, a drone can automatically launch to track the target from the sky. This provides continuous real-time video feeds and location data to authorities long before patrol cars can even get to the area.

Now that we know what the outside looks like, let's take a look inside. I think if it's allowed to see us, it's only fair we see how it sees us.

They're in my head.

The Falcon cameras use a Wake-On-Motion loop paired with an AI Object Detection model. Instead of recording all day and burning through data, the camera stays in a low-power state until it detects changing pixels in its field of view. Once triggered, it snaps pictures, utilizes onboard machine learning for visual processing, and connects to a cloud tool called Flock Freeform to log your car and all its details.

Flock Condor is where things get silly. The Condor live streams straight to Flock FreeForm and FlockOS, so local subscribers can use FreeForm as a kind of surveillance Google to search for places, cars, and people. The Condor pairs a Pan-Tilt-Zoom lens with a 25x optical servo-driven zoom and computer vision algorithms like YOLO to draw bounding boxes around a subject. Most software like this then generates a center point for the box which becomes a tracking point. The PTZ camera's motors physically follow that point until another "relevant target" is in frame, logging everything in the process like clothing, pace, location, yada yada. Some supermarkets use the same tech to follow you around the store and change prices to squeeze you for all your money, but that'll be another article altogether.

The Eyes Off Cedar Rapids team posted a great breakdown of the internal workings of the Flock Falcon camera, revealing that the computer vision model running is YOLO_PICO3_FLOAT16 paired with OpenCV. Before the onboard AI can figure out what it’s looking at, it needs to be able to look at all. That is where OpenCV (Open Source Computer Vision) comes in. OpenCV is the world's most popular open-source software library for handling video and images. Think of it as the eyes and optical nerve.

Once OpenCV crops and hands over a clean picture, the AI brain takes over. That brain architecture is called YOLO (You Only Look Once). In milliseconds, it draws a bounding box around a shape and says: "There is a 98% chance that box contains a car, and a 94% chance that box contains a pedestrian." The PICO3_FLOAT16 part means it is an incredibly shrunken, mathematically rounded AI brain designed to fit on low-end local hardware. Because the AI is so small and uses lightweight math, it near-instantly identifies the vehicle type, color, and license plate right there on the pole before sending it to the cloud.

This in a nutshell is what it sees. I put together a visual aid tool called Dead Bird to help us see how the surveillance state views us day to day. I built it using open-source software I found on GitHub, mainly YOLO11n and RapidOCR for the plate reader. To simulate the Condor, I'm using yolov8n-pose, OpenPAR, and DeepFace. PAR stands for Pedestrian Attribute Recognition, which means it tries to detect a person's attributes and generates a text list of descriptors. DeepFace is a software that can attempt to isolate facial features and guess age, gender, and emotions.

Note that I cut some corners on the DeepFace and OpenPAR training data to save some storage, and in turn they kinda suck. While I can't follow myself around or log a city's worth of data, I do think this serves as a decent visual tool to understand exactly how we are being watched.

Another terrifying thing about a nationwide database accessible by a username and password is the allure of abusing a system this powerful.

  • Milwaukee, Wisconsin (2026): Police Officer Josue Ayala resigned and was criminally charged with misconduct in public office after abusing his department's Flock access. He looked up the location of a woman he was dating, as well as her ex-boyfriend, nearly 180 times over a two-month period.

  • Costa Mesa, California (2026): Officer Robert Josett pleaded guilty to criminal charges after an internal investigation revealed he had weaponized the local Flock camera network to track the daily movements of his mistress and her other romantic interests.

  • Sedgwick, Kansas (2023): Police Chief Lee Nygaard was forced to resign after an audit showed he used Flock cameras to actively track his ex-girlfriend and her new partner more than 200 times over the span of several months.

  • EFF Audit (2025): An in-depth analysis of Flock Safety audit logs conducted by the EFF revealed that over 80 law enforcement agencies used the nationwide search function to target minority communities based entirely on racial stereotypes. Officers performed hundreds of bulk searches using terms like "roma," "roma traveler," and anti-Romani slurs, often without associating the search with any actual active crime or case number.

  • Denver, Colorado / Northern California (2026): Cities like Denver, Ithaca, and San Jose faced major public blowback and massive class-action privacy lawsuits after internal investigations proved local police were using Flock networks to bypass sanctuary city laws. Local officers were executing searches on behalf of federal ICE agents to track undocumented immigrants. Because Flock shares a unified cloud network, federal agencies were quietly gaining backdoor access to local municipal data. This scandal caused at least 30 cities to abruptly cancel their multi-million dollar contracts with Flock in early 2026 alone.
